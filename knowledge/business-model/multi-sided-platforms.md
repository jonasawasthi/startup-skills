# Multi-sided platforms and network effects

See also: [network effects (NFX summary)](../other/network-effects.md), [a16z defensibility](../a16z/defensibility.md), [marketplace business models](marketplace-business-models.md).

## Attributed (titles and scope only)

- **NFX** catalogues many distinct types of network effect in its *Network Effects Manual* and *Network Effects Bible*; an earlier summary is in [other/network-effects.md](../other/network-effects.md). Neither page was re-read for this file.
- **Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne (HBR, 2006)** wrote on pricing and strategy in two-sided markets; **Van Alstyne, Parker and Choudary (HBR, 2016)** on platforms versus pipelines. Both are paywalled and were not read; they are listed as further reading, not summarised.

## Original synthesis

- **Cross-side vs same-side.** A cross-side effect means more of one side makes the platform more valuable to the other (more teachers → more choice for learners). A same-side effect means more of the same side helps (or hurts, through competition) that side. Name which one the model relies on.
- **Network effects are a hypothesis until measured.** Evidence looks like rising match or completion rates, or falling acquisition cost per participant, as a defined market fills up. A growing registration count is not evidence.
- **Multi-sided pricing.** Usually one side is subsidised (free or discounted) because its presence attracts the side that pays. Record which side is subsidised, why, and the assumption that the subsidy pays back.
- **Audience-led platforms are different.** When each supplier brings their own audience (creators, teachers), demand may follow suppliers rather than the platform, so cross-side effects can be weak and leakage strong. Test whether buyers discover suppliers through the platform at all.
