# Customer interview methodology

## Attributed

- **The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick).** The book's premise is that people will say your idea is good to be kind. Useful questions are about the customer's life and past behaviour, not your idea. Compliments and hypothetical promises are not data. Commitments are: time, reputation or money.
- **First Round Review with Michael Sippey (2015).** Lessons from his career:
  - talk to customers constantly, and hold roughly 30 meetings with the people who would buy, *before* writing code;
  - spend most of each meeting on the problem (whether they have it, how they solve it now, what it costs), and only then on the proposed solution;
  - bring the whole team, and debrief and score each meeting immediately; patterns emerge over the first 10 to 30 meetings;
  - stay open to finding that you have the wrong buyer.
- **YC Library.** Alströmer's talk covers how to talk to users. Caldwell and Seibel discuss how spending real time with users unlocks insight and growth.

## How the skill applies this (original synthesis)

**Question rules** (enforced by `startup-kit discovery check` on interview guides):

| Avoid | Why | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use / buy / pay for…?" | Hypothetical; people are optimistic | "When did you last pay for something to solve this? How much?" |
| "Don't you think… / Wouldn't it be great…?" | Leading; invites agreement | "How do you handle this today?" |
| "Do you like our idea / app?" | Pitches; invites compliments | "What's the hardest part of [job]?" |
| Two questions in one | Muddles the answer | One question at a time |
| Yes/no questions as the main question | Closed; little to learn | "Tell me about…", "Walk me through…" |

**Interview structure:**

1. Context (role, situation).
2. The last time the problem happened, told as a story.
3. Current alternatives and workarounds.
4. Spend, in money and time, and who decided.
5. What they have tried, and why it fell short.
6. A commitment ask: a follow-up, an introduction, a pilot or a deposit.

**Notes** separate *verbatim statements* (quotes, ideally checked against a transcript) from *observations* (what the interviewer saw) and *interpretation* (what it might mean). Only the first two are evidence.

**Sample-size heuristic.** Many practitioners report that themes start to repeat after roughly 10–15 interviews per segment. Sippey describes patterns emerging over 10 to 30 meetings. These are practical heuristics, not statistical standards. Interviews find problems and language; they don't measure prevalence.
