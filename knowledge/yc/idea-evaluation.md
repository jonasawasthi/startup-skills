# Evaluating startup ideas

## Four common mistakes (Friedman)

1. **Waiting for a brilliant idea.** Great ideas only look brilliant in hindsight. Start with a good one and iterate.
2. **Jumping on the first idea without critical thought.**
3. **Starting with a solution instead of a problem** ("a solution in search of a problem", e.g. "Uber for X" with no evidence anyone needs it).
4. **Believing ideas are hard to find.** Real problems are everywhere. Filters hide them.

## Evaluation criteria (Friedman, building on Dalton Caldwell's pivoting talk)

| Criterion | Question the evaluator should ask |
|---|---|
| Size | Are there already large companies doing something similar? Could this be big if it works? |
| Founder–market fit | Are the founders unusually well suited to this exact problem? |
| Problem certainty | How sure are we that this is a real, important problem? Is there personal experience of it? |
| New insight | Do the founders know something important that others don't? |
| Personal want | Is this something the founders themselves want? |
| Recency | Did something only recently become possible (technology, regulation, behaviour)? |
| Proxy | Is there a successful company doing something similar elsewhere (a different market or segment)? |
| Longevity | Would the founders work on it for years? |
| Scalability | Can it grow without linear human effort, eventually? |
| Idea space | Is the surrounding space fertile, so that a pivot nearby could still work? |

## Bad filters (reasons people wrongly reject ideas)

- **Hard to start** ("schlep blindness", Graham, with Stripe as the example).
- **Boring space**, e.g. payroll (Gusto).
- **Too ambitious.** Ambitious ideas can attract talent and capital more easily than incremental ones.
- **Existing competitors.** Competition is often evidence of a market. What matters is the insight.

## Seven recipes for generating ideas (Friedman)

1. Use expertise from work you've done. Friedman describes this as the strongest recipe, the one behind many of YC's most successful companies.
2. Things you wish someone would build for you.
3. What you'd work on for ten years even if it failed.
4. Recent changes in the world (technology, platforms, regulation, new problems).
5. New variants of recently successful companies.
6. Crowdsourcing problems from people with domain expertise.
7. Industries that seem broken.

## How Startup Skills applies this

The *startup-idea-evaluator* scores each criterion **qualitatively**, with evidence and a confidence level. These criteria are heuristics drawn from YC's pattern-matching across many companies. They do not predict any individual company's success, and the evaluator never converts them into a probability.

## Limitations

These heuristics are drawn from a portfolio dominated by software startups in the US. Founder–market fit and "personal want" can mislead in B2B or emerging-market contexts, where the founder is not the user. In those cases, use direct customer evidence instead.
