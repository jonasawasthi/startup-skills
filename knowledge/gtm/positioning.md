# Positioning

## Attributed: Dunford's components (paraphrased)

Dunford treats positioning as the context that tells customers what a product is, who it is for and why it is better than what they use today. Her quickstart guide (2021) describes five linked components, worked through **in this order**:

1. **Competitive alternatives**: what the customer would do if the product did not exist. Often this is a spreadsheet, a manual process, a person they hire, or doing nothing.
2. **Differentiated capabilities**: what the product does that those alternatives cannot.
3. **Value**: what those capabilities let the customer achieve, expressed as an outcome.
4. **Best-fit customers**: the customers who care most about that value. Not everyone who *could* use the product.
5. **Market category**: the frame of reference that makes the value obvious to those customers. It is an *output* of the work, not the starting point.

Her warnings, as described in the guide:

- Fill-in-the-blank positioning statements assume one right answer per box and can mislead a team.
- Position against the alternatives customers actually consider, not "phantom competitors" who never show up in a decision.
- The status quo ("do nothing") is often the real competitor.
- Creating a new category is expensive. Many winners started in an existing category and redefined it later.
- Positioning is not messaging, a tagline or a vision. Messaging is built *on top of* positioning.

## Attributed: Moore's beachhead framing

In *Crossing the Chasm* (1991, revised 1999 and 2014), Moore argues that pragmatic mainstream buyers behave differently from early adopters, and that a company crosses the gap by dominating one narrow segment first. That segment then becomes a reference base for adjacent segments. Moore's widely cited positioning-statement template (target customer, need, category, key benefit, primary alternative, key differentiator) is a compact way to *record* a positioning decision.

## How the GTM skill applies this (original synthesis)

1. **Work the components in Dunford's order** and cite evidence for each one. Alternatives come from interviews or observed behaviour, capabilities from the codebase or product (`status` must be live or built), and value from customer evidence or a labelled hypothesis.
2. **Use a statement as a record, not as the method.** The skill writes a Moore-style statement *after* the components are settled, and labels it an internal artefact. That respects Dunford's warning while giving teams a one-line summary.
3. **No unsupported differentiation.** A capability counts as differentiated only if (a) it exists (`live` or `built`) and (b) there is evidence that the named alternatives lack it. Otherwise it is listed as a *hypothesised* differentiator, with a validation task.
4. **Segment-specific messaging** inherits from one positioning. Each segment gets its own pain, proof and call to action, but the category and core value stay the same.
5. **Feature → benefit → proof.** Every feature-to-benefit line has a proof column: an evidence ID, or the word *hypothesis*.

## Limitations

Dunford's practice centres on B2B technology. Consumer and creator products often position through community, identity and price as much as through capability. Moore's model assumes a technology-adoption curve that may not apply to simple consumer products.
